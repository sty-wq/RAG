## cross-v1-04

In machine translation of occupations, how did the Google Translate pronoun study diagnose gender imbalance, and what training set did the domain-adaptation study construct to address it?

The Google Translate study diagnosed gender imbalance by building sentences such as “He/She is an Engineer” in 12 gender-neutral languages (e.g., Hungarian, Chinese, Yoruba), translating them into English with the Google Translate API, and collecting statistics on the frequency of female, male, and gender-neutral pronouns in the output; it then compared these frequencies against U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data on women's participation in each job position, finding that GT's male defaults overestimated the real-world male share and that female pronoun frequencies under-estimated female participation [1]. The domain-adaptation study constructed a tiny, handcrafted, gender-balanced profession dataset: sentences following the template “The [PROFESSION] finished [his|her] work,” using 194 professions drawn from the list collected from US labour statistics (with field-specific adjectives removed), giving 388 sentences in a gender-balanced set, with masculine and feminine templates manually translated for each target language; it also created a no-overlap variant removing professions occurring in WinoMT (216 sentences, padded back to 388 with balanced adjective-based sentences) [2].

A significant number of AI tools have recently been suggested to be harmfully biased towards some minority, with reports of racist criminal behavior predictors, Iphone X failing to differentiate between two Asian people and Google photos' mistakenly classifying black people as gorillas. Although a systematic study of such biases can be difficult, we believe that automated translation tools can be exploited through gender neutral languages to yield a window into the phenomenon of gender bias in AI. In this paper, we start with a comprehensive list of job positions from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and used it to build sentences in constructions like"He/She is an Engineer"in 12 different gender neutral languages such as Hungarian, Chinese, Yoruba, and several others. We translate these sentences into English using the Google Translate API, and collect statistics about the frequency of female, male and gender-neutral pronouns in the translated output. We show that GT exhibits a strong tendency towards male defaults, in particular for fields linked to unbalanced gender distribution such as STEM jobs. If training a dialogue system on SAMSum and Interview, what kind of supervision can it take from each corpus, and at what granularity?

At what level is query–candidate similarity computed in content-based web table retrieval versus semantic PubMed query matching, and what signal does each use?

In content-based web table retrieval, query–candidate similarity in the first retrieval step is computed at the **bag-of-words level**: the query is represented as bag-of-words and the table as plain text composed of words from caption and headers, with similarity calculated using Okapi BM25 [1]. The signal used in the table retrieval step is BM25 term-matching over caption/header words [1]; the signal used in the PubMed method is neural word-embedding semantic relatedness between query words and document words [2].
